Music & Dyslexia

Reading language and reading music turn out to share more machinery than they seem to.

What dyslexia affects

Dyslexia is often described as a reading difficulty, but the underlying differences run deeper: symbol decoding, sequencing, directionality, working memory, and processing speed. Those same underlying skills turn out to matter just as much for reading music.

Where the two overlap

  • Symbol decoding — a note on a line means a specific pitch and duration, the same way a letter means a specific sound. Both require converting an abstract mark into meaning, quickly and automatically.
  • Sequencing — both text and notation unfold left to right, moment to moment, and losing your place has a similar cost in either system.
  • Directionality — readers with dyslexia often show weaker left-to-right tracking; music notation requires that same tracking, plus a second, vertical axis for pitch.
  • Working memory — holding a symbol's meaning in mind while decoding the next one taxes the same short-term memory system in both reading and music.
  • Processing speed — fluency in both domains depends on decoding becoming fast and automatic; for many people with dyslexia, it stays effortful in both.

Where music reading is different

Music notation isn't just a second alphabet. It encodes two dimensions at once — pitch (vertical position) and rhythm (horizontal spacing and symbol shape) — and many notes look nearly identical except for their placement on the staff. Reading music also frequently demands two independent streams at once, as when a pianist reads separate lines for each hand.

What researchers know — and don't yet

There isn't a large body of research specifically on music-reading difficulty, especially compared to the research base for dyslexia. What exists suggests a real relationship worth taking seriously, along with promising anecdotal and small-study evidence that multisensory teaching helps. What's still unclear: how many people are affected, whether it's best understood as one condition or several overlapping ones, and how much the difficulty is about notation specifically versus broader auditory or attentional differences.
