Multisensory Activities

Concrete things to try, organized by what they target. Some cost nothing; a few pair well with printable materials.

  • Flash a single note; the student names it and finds it on the instrument before moving on.
  • Color-code noteheads to matching colored stickers on the instrument, then gradually fade the color.
  • "Landmark note" method — anchor a few easy-to-spot notes (like the lines that touch a ledger line) and reason outward from them instead of naming every note from scratch.
  • Clap or step a rhythm by ear first, then look at how it's written afterward, so notation confirms something already felt.
  • Use rhythm syllables (ta, ta-di) spoken aloud while pointing to each note.
  • Body percussion call-and-response, gradually mapping each sound to its written duration.
  • Trace the shape of a melodic line with a finger before playing it, narrating "up, up, down" aloud.
  • Use a floor staff and physically walk pitch direction for short phrases.
  • Hand-signing pitch height alongside singing, before introducing the written line.
  • Matching games pairing a note name, its staff position, and its instrument location as three cards to connect.
  • "What changed?" — play a two-measure phrase twice with one note altered; the student identifies the change by ear.
  • Short mnemonic phrases for line and space names, said aloud until automatic.
  • Run a finger or a card under the line being read to anchor the eye's place.
  • Enlarge spacing between staff lines and notes on practice copies to reduce visual crowding.
  • Practice re-finding a spot after intentionally looking away, to build recovery skills for real playing.
  • Teach a short phrase entirely by ear first; introduce the printed version only once it's already secure.
  • Use colored tape or stickers at key finger positions, tied to colored noteheads.
  • Keep early reading material short — a few notes at a time — so decoding doesn't overwhelm playing.
  • Lead with recordings and ear-based learning, treating notation as a reference rather than the primary route in.
  • Use notation software that enlarges, recolors, or simplifies a score to reduce visual load.
  • Name the pattern explicitly to a teacher — "I read slowly, but I learn quickly by ear" — so lessons can be structured around that strength from the start.
