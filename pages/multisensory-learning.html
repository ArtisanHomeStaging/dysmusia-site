Multisensory Learning

When reading notation in isolation doesn't work, routing the same information through other senses often does.

Multisensory instruction is well established for language-based dyslexia — approaches like Orton-Gillingham are built entirely around it. The same logic applies to music: instead of relying on sight-reading alone, these approaches route the same musical information through color, movement, sound, story, and touch, giving the brain more than one path to the same meaning.

ColorVisual pattern

Assigning a consistent color to each note name or string turns an abstract symbol into a visual pattern the eye can catch quickly.

  • Color-coded strings or keys matched to color-coded noteheads
  • A "Colour Staff" system, where lines and spaces are tinted rather than plain black
  • Highlighting repeating visual landmarks (the same measure, the same interval shape) in a consistent color
MovementBody mapping

Mapping pitch or rhythm onto the body or a physical space turns an abstract relationship into something felt.

  • A floor staff large enough to walk on, stepping "up" and "down" as pitch rises and falls
  • Conducting or clapping patterns that mirror note duration before ever reading it
  • Hand-signing pitch height (a system borrowed from Kodály-style teaching) alongside the printed note
SoundListening first

Hearing a passage before reading it gives the ear a target to match the page against, instead of decoding cold.

  • Listening to a short phrase on repeat before attempting to read it
  • Singing a line by ear, then locating it on the staff afterward
  • Call-and-response rhythm games that build a felt sense of timing before notation is introduced
StoryImagination & narrative

Attaching a short story or image to a musical pattern gives working memory something more durable to hold onto than an abstract symbol.

  • Mnemonic phrases for line and space note names
  • Character-based stories for recurring rhythmic patterns ("the running notes," "the resting note")
  • Verbal patterns or chants that mirror a rhythm's shape
TouchPhysical interaction

Physically manipulating notation — rather than only looking at it — adds a motor memory trace to a visual one.

  • Tracing notation with a finger while naming it aloud
  • Building phrases from movable, tactile note cards before writing them
  • Finger-position landmarks on an instrument tied to specific staff positions
Where these ideas come from

Multisensory approaches to music notation have been developed independently by several music-and-dyslexia specialists — including Margaret Hubicki's Colour Staff system, developed in the 1970s before multisensory literacy teaching was widely known in the UK.
