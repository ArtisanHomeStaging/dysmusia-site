A History of Music-Reading Difficulties

Not just the history of a word — the longer story of how researchers came to notice this pattern at all.

1970s

Early multisensory notation teaching

Margaret Hubicki develops the Colour Staff system for teaching notation, years before multisensory literacy instruction became widely recognized in the UK.

1980

Early prevalence estimates

Kalmus and Fry publish early estimates distinguishing self-reported "tone-deafness" from measured perceptual difficulty with music, laying groundwork for later prevalence research.

1990s–2000s

Congenital amusia research develops

Isabelle Peretz, Julie Ayotte, and colleagues at the University of Montreal study adults with lifelong musical-listening difficulty, establishing it as a distinct, measurable perceptual pattern rather than a vague self-description.

2000

"Musical dyslexia" proposed

Retired pediatric neurologist Dr. Neil Gordon coins the term, explicitly drawing the parallel to dyslexia and dyscalculia.

2000s

Music-and-dyslexia specialists collect case studies

Music teachers and language therapists — including T. R. Miles and John Westcombe, editors of Music and Dyslexia — begin compiling case studies and early research specifically on notation-reading difficulty in dyslexic musicians.

2010s–present

Growing but still thin research base

Interest in the overlap between dyslexia and music grows among educators, but the research base remains small relative to language-based dyslexia — leaving most working knowledge in the hands of specialist teachers rather than large studies.

Dysmusia

An independent, informational project exploring why some capable musicians struggle to read music — and finding better ways to teach them. Not a diagnostic tool or medical advice.
